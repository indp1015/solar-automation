How to Size Your Solar System (Without Oversizing)

Sizing your solar system correctly is the most important decision you'll make — more important than panel brand or inverter choice. Here's how to do it without wasting money.

Step 1: Calculate annual usage

Look at your last 12 utility bills. Add up the kWh used. Example: 10,500 kWh/year for a typical U.S. home.

If you're planning additions (EV, heat pump, new addition), add 20-30% to that number. EVs typically add 3,000-4,500 kWh/year.

Step 2: Estimate production per kW

Your county's peak sun hours determine annual production per kW installed. Formula:

Annual kWh per kW = peak sun hours/day × 365 × 0.80 (performance ratio)

  • Phoenix (5.5 hrs/day): ~1,600 kWh/kW
  • Los Angeles (5.0 hrs/day): ~1,460 kWh/kW
  • Chicago (3.8 hrs/day): ~1,110 kWh/kW
  • Seattle (3.1 hrs/day): ~905 kWh/kW

Step 3: Match to your usage

For a 10,500 kWh home in Los Angeles:

10,500 ÷ 1,460 = 7.2 kW system

Round to the nearest inverter size (7 kW or 7.5 kW). Our calculator lets you test 4kW-10kW scenarios.

Step 4: Check roof space

A typical 400W panel is roughly 5.5 ft × 3.5 ft = 19.25 sq ft. A 7.5kW system needs:

7,500 ÷ 400 = 19 panels × 19.25 sq ft = ~365 sq ft of unshaded roof

Most single-family homes have 400-600 sq ft of south-facing roof — sufficient for 7-10 kW. If shade is an issue, consider microinverters (which minimize per-panel shading losses).

Why oversizing is now a costly mistake

Pre-2020, oversizing was often smart: excess generation was fully credited at retail, so extra panels were pure profit. In 2026:

  • NEM 3.0 (CA): excess exports earn only 25% of retail — oversizing exports valuable kWh for cheap.
  • Reduced-credit states: same problem — excess generation is worth less than what you paid to install it.
  • Battery cost: storing excess in batteries helps, but adds $800-$1,200/kWh battery cost — usually more than the extra solar itself.

Rule of thumb: size to 90-100% of annual usage. Oversizing by 20%+ is now usually a waste of $3,000-$5,000.

Undersizing can also be smart

In a 1:1 net metering state, size to 100-120% of usage — credit bank pays out year-end. In reduced-credit states, size to 90% and let grid supply the rest. The smaller system earns the same per-kWh savings on the first 90%; the last 10% is inefficient.

When to add a battery

Add 5-10 kWh battery storage if:

  • You're in a NEM 3.0 or reduced-credit state
  • You have high evening TOU rates (2x peak)
  • Frequent outages in your area

Skip if 1:1 net metering is available and outages are rare — $10,000 for a battery that doesn't pay back.

Bottom line

Calculate annual usage → divide by your county's peak sun × 0.80 → round up to nearest inverter size. Verify roof space and shade. In most of the U.S., a 6-8 kW system covers a typical home. Our county pages show the exact payback for any size from 4kW to 10kW.
